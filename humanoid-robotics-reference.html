Post 1 — Crossing the Chasm

Post 2 — The Assumption That Just Got Flipped (Exoskeletons)

For years, the field assumed rigid back exoskeletons offload the spine more than soft exosuits. It made intuitive sense on paper — simple physics, mechanical leverage, perpendicular force application. One problem: no one had actually tested it. A new peer-reviewed study out of Germany just did. The finding? For most participants, the soft exosuit reduced spinal compression force more than the rigid device. This is science doing what it should — exposing assumptions baked into the literature before they calcified into design dogma. It also reinforces something I think about a lot: human biomechanics is rarely as tidy as our models. Muscle dynamics, movement patterns, and real-world mechanics routinely make intuition break down. The field is better for having this data. And better for being willing to be wrong. [ADD YOUR ANGLE — does this connect to your clinical or research experience?]

Post 3 — The Hand Problem

Humanoid robots can walk. Some can run. A few can do backflips. What they still can't do reliably: pick up a screwdriver. The hand — dexterous, contact-rich, force-sensitive manipulation — is now the gating constraint for the entire humanoid industry. Locomotion is largely a solved problem. Grasping isn't. This gap matters because most of the value in physical labor isn't in moving from point A to point B. It's in what you do when you get there. Until robots can reliably interact with the unstructured, irregular, unpredictable physical world through their hands, the commercial case stays narrow. Post 4 — Hype Harm (AI + Robotics)

An 18-year-old recently wrote to a researcher asking if there was any point pursuing a career in AI — worried that by the time he graduated, the work would be done. That question stuck with me. Hype doesn't just mislead investors. It discourages the next generation of builders. The truth is more nuanced: modern AI is genuinely remarkable and genuinely limited. LLMs still require significant customization to perform well on specific tasks. Robots still struggle with manipulation. AGI — if it comes — is likely decades away. The gap between what AI can do in demos and what it can do reliably, at scale, in the messy real world is still enormous. And closing that gap is exactly the kind of work that needs people. Encourage the 18-year-olds. The hard problems are just getting started. [YOUR FRAMING — what would you say to students entering this space?]

Post 5 — NeurIPS 2025 / Embodied AI State of the Field

A few things stood out from the embodied AI work at NeurIPS 2025: Continual learning — the ability to actually learn from experience without forgetting — still largely doesn't work. That's a foundational gap. Imitation learning from teleoperation has plateaued, much like supervised fine-tuning on text. The field is increasingly looking to RL. World models are getting powerful, but manipulation (contact-rich tasks) remains far harder than locomotion or driving. And inference speed? No longer the bottleneck — new tokenization and caching approaches have largely solved it. The picture that emerges: locomotion is maturing fast. Manipulation, long-horizon planning, and real-world generalization are still wide open. That's not a reason for pessimism. It's a map of where the interesting work is.
